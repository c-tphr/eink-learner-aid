The Keeper's Log

[d04-p002] paragraph, 73 words, id c3-p002
When spring came, Dr. Vane began to copy the old logbooks into a simple list so that she could study them. Ione had kept the log for nineteen years, 1907– 1925, and its pages held the time, the weather and the state of the light for every hour of every night. Some pages were stained with salt water, and a few were so faded that she had to read them by the window.

[d04-p003] paragraph, 40 words, id c3-p003
The doctor wanted to know how often the fog signal had been needed. Counting by hand would take weeks, so she wrote a short program instead. It read one line per hour and added up the hours marked as fog.

The answer surprised them both. Over nineteen years the horn had sounded for more than four thousand hours, and nearly half of those hours fell in November and December. Why had nobody noticed before? Ione said that a keeper only ever sees one night at a time.

[d04-p006] paragraph, 53 words, id c3-p006
She also counted the nights when the clockwork had stopped. There were only three in nineteen years. Each time, Ione had climbed into the lamp room and turned the great lens by hand until dawn, walking it round and round so that passing ships would still see one white flash every ten seconds.

[d04-p007] paragraph, 40 words, id c3-p007
Dr. Vane sent her figures to the lighthouse board with a request for a second engine and a larger coal store. The reply came in June: the board agreed, and the new engine would arrive before the next fog season.

[d04-p008] paragraph, 40 words, id c3-p008
On her last evening on the rock, the doctor climbed the steps once more and watched Ione light the lamp. The beam swept out across the strait, one white flash every ten seconds, exactly as it had for nineteen years.
